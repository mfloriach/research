# Introduction

## What is {site.brand}?

{site.brand} is a structured argument-analysis workspace. It reads media
reporting side by side with a structured audit of the underlying claim, so a
single thesis — currently a worked example on carbon border taxes — can be
examined piece by piece instead of as one blurred block of prose.

A short description of the idea is in the site subtitle: climate and economy
reporting, read against a structured audit of the carbon border tax argument.
Everything a reader sees is built from two content models:

- **Reporting articles.** The left column lists the dossier's reporting as
  articles, each split into paragraphs. An article is either text, or a YouTube
  video, or both — a video embed keeps the same paragraph structure so its
  claims can be audited the same way. Articles carry a title, labels, an
  optional byline (author, ISO date, author wallet address), and a view count.
- **Audit items.** The right column lists audit items, each classified into
  one of five tabs: Contraargument, Fallacies, Evidences, Sources, and
  Interpretation. Every item is a peer entry in the same registry, not a
  comment attached to a document.

The two columns are linked by IDs. A reporting paragraph declares which audit
items it invokes through an `auditItemIds` array. Clicking a paragraph keeps
the audit column on that subset of items, and clicking an item makes the
paragraph that references it stand out. That bidirectional link is the core of
the product: a reader never has to guess which evidence supports which claim,
or which claim a piece of evidence was raised for.

## Core capabilities

### Paragraph-level audit linking

The link between a claim and its supporting material is made explicit at the
paragraph, not the article level. Each `ReportingParagraph` references a set
of audit-item IDs, so the audit column always reflects the exact footprint of
the text in front of the reader. Selecting a paragraph resizes the audit
panel's tab list to only the items relevant to that paragraph, and the audit
items are resolved by title from the content index so that IDs never appear in
the interface.

### Five audit categories

Every audit item lives in one of five fixed categories, defined in
`AUDIT_TABS`. Each answers a different question about a claim:

| Category        | What it captures                                            |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Contraargument  | A direct rebuttal of the thesis.                            |
| Fallacies       | A logical problem with how the argument is built.           |
| Evidences       | A fact, figure, or result that supports or undercuts the claim. |
| Sources         | The publication the claim traces back to.                  |
| Interpretation  | A reasoned reading of what the material means.              |

Because the categories are fixed, audits stay comparable across topics, and
the UI can give each a stable tab, badge colour, and sort axis.

### On-chain attestations

Audit items can be attested by wallets through an EVM smart contract,
`AttestationRegistry`. The contract enforces one attestation per wallet per
item — a repeat call reverts with `AlreadyAttested` — and maintains a running
count per item. Reading is cheap and free: `attestationCount` returns the total
and `hasAttested` reports whether the connected wallet has vouched for that
item. Writes go through the wallet, so an attestation can never be forged and
can always be traced back to the signer. The count is shown on every audit
card as a shield badge.

### Wallet signatures and provenance

Creating a report or an audit item also signs it. The wallet signs the exact
payload, and the contract records the content hash, the signature, and the
item's IPFS CID through `recordSignature`, one record per wallet per item.
Putting the CID on-chain anchors the canonical JSON payload pinned to IPFS so
its location cannot be swapped after the fact. The `/debate/provenance` page
reconstructs a wallet's full history from the `Attested` and `ItemProvenance`
events, in block order, and article bylines link there so a reader can jump
from an author's name to every attestation and signature they have recorded.

### Vector search with an LLM answer

`/api/search` powers the dossier search. It embeds the query with a local
sentence model (MiniLM, 384 dimensions, CPU-only, downloaded once and cached)
and runs a vector-similarity search over article embeddings stored in
MongoDB's Atlas Vector Search. The top similarity score decides whether a
match exists, and an LLM provider writes a short answer that cites the
matching articles. So a question like "does leakage actually occur?" returns
both an explanation and the supporting articles it was grounded in.

### Media-friendly reporting

A reporting article is not limited to text. It can be a YouTube video (parsed
from `watch`, `youtu.be`, `embed`, or `shorts` URLs), text, or both. The video
embeds in the card body alongside the same paragraph list, so a recorded
argument can still be audited claim by claim. Text articles render their
paragraphs as directly clickable blocks for the audit hand-off; video articles
reserve their paragraphs for the same linking.

### Filtering by format, label, and date

The reporting panel can be narrowed without losing context. Choose **Format**
to see text, video, or everything; toggle **Labels** to restrict to one or
more topics (labels combine with OR); or change the **Date** axis from
editorial order to newest or oldest, with articles missing a date always
sorting last. The active filter is encoded in the query string, so a filtered
view can be shared as a link, and when a filter set eliminates every tab the
panel says so explicitly rather than going blank.

## Use cases

**Policy teams under a tight deadline.** A team drafting a position on carbon
pricing can open one thesis and immediately see every upstream source and the
counterarguments that survive or fail, instead of trusting a chain of
summaries in a shared document.

**Reviewers and editors.** An editor can check whether a statistic's
attribution survives scrutiny by following the `Sources` entries and the
attestation counts, and can flag fallacies in a draft before publication.
Because attestations are on-chain, an editor can tell at a glance which claims
have already been reviewed by a connected account and which have not.

**Researchers and students.** A researcher evaluating the carbon-border-tax
literature can use provenance to see *who* vouched for *which* claim, and can
compare the reported evidence against the interpretations that cite it, keeping
the distinction between a result and its reading explicit.

**Fact-checkers.** The five categories map onto a checklist: does the claim
hold up to its contraarguments, are the fallacies real, is the evidence
self-contained, does the source actually support the wording used, and is the
interpretation fair? It is a useful scaffold for any dispute about a claim.

**Transparent publishing.** Newsletters or policy shops that publish produces
can pin their full payload to IPFS, have authors sign it, and expose the
provenance trail to readers — a way to say not just *what* was claimed, but
*that the claim has been reported and endorsed by identifiable accounts.*

## Is {site.brand} for me?

{site.brand} is for you if you need to reason about a claim and its support at
the level of individual paragraphs, want every assertion traceable to a typed
audit item, and care that attestations and authorship are attributable rather
than anonymous. It is a good fit for policy analysis, fact-checking workflows,
editorial review, and teaching the distinction between evidence and
interpretation.

It is not a good fit for everyone. You likely do not want this tool if:

- You need a free-form discussion or comment thread. Everything is a typed
  audit item, so freeform back-and-forth is not the right shape.
- You need real-time collaborative editing. Content is assembled per article
  and per item, not co-edited live.
- Your claim is genuinely multimodal or does not decompose into discrete
  paragraphs. It rewards text and video that can be segmented into claims.
- You are not comfortable with a wallet. Every meaningful action — creating a
  report, creating an audit item, attesting, or signing — is performed by a
  connected wallet; without one, read-only consumption is all the product
  offers.
